## Suggested Discussion text

### A distributional species trait has a geographic allocation

Species-wide colour variation is not distributed interchangeably among photographed localities. In three species-disjoint high-depth resources, nearby conspecific photographs were more homogeneous than the corresponding species-wide pools, even when null assignments preserved the exact colour composition of each species. At 50 km, species-average local versus range-wide pairwise colour discordance was 0.2009 versus 0.2214 in discovery, 0.2100 versus 0.2287 in validation and 0.2335 versus 0.2482 in the third cohort (p = 0.005 in each test). These correspond to local-to-range-wide photographic discordance ratios of approximately 90.7%, 91.8% and 94.1%. This means that local photographic pairs retain much of the range-wide **pairwise colour dissimilarity**, but are nonetheless reproducibly more homogeneous than composition-matched null worlds. It does not mean that 91–94% of genetic flower-colour variance is maintained within natural populations: 50-km neighbourhoods are neither breeding populations nor independent plants.

That modest geographic deficit replicated across several distance scales and remained in controls involving distinct observers, colour states other than white and continuous nine-colour representations. Together, these findings distinguish overall species-wide intraspecific trait variation (ITV) from its allocation across localities. A species-wide colour-diversity index can be substantial when photographs from different places represent different colour compositions, when locally photographed alternatives coexist, or when both processes operate. Inference about the maintenance of genetic variants requires independent plants and fitness or genotype data, but the observed geographic allocation identifies a widespread pattern that any such explanation must accommodate. Spatially varying selection is a plausible generator, as are restricted dispersal, colonisation history, drift and plasticity.

### A distinct, prospectively confirmed regularity concerns phenotype space

A separate and more specifically protected result identifies the direction of variation in photographed colour space. Discovery and validation analyses localized recurrent continuous colour displacement to the achromatic–chromatic, white-versus-nonwhite contrast. Having identified that direction after the initial data had been opened, the target axis, threshold, admissibility conditions and structured null were frozen before testing the separately acquired third cohort. Among 158 newly tested eligible species, mean squared alignment was W = 0.517 against a coarse-state-preserving structured-null median of 0.457 (p = 0.001). Removing that contrast left no supported shared non-white hue axis.

The qualification is consequential. An image-based four-state colour representation already builds some white/nonwhite distinction into the available geometry; the frozen null controls that compositional construction. The replicated quantity is therefore the *excess* alignment above this baseline, not a discovery that white and non-white states themselves exist. Moreover, colour near-clipping is associated with source image white labels. Although strong highlight exclusion retains an excess axis, that technical sensitivity does not independently authenticate the anatomical pigment state of photographed petals. The reliable observation is a repeatedly measured phenotype-space direction, not a measured shared mutation, pigment loss or fitness benefit.

The juxtaposition of these findings suggests that recurrent properties of phenotypic variation need not imply recurrent geographic boundaries. The same broadly aligned contrast can be expressed at different positions within different photographed species distributions. Conversely, similar local-versus-range-wide discordance does not require the same continuous hue displacement. Thus the questions of *which phenotypes change* and *where their distributions change* should be posed and tested separately. Our results establish a recurrent measured axis and widespread geographic allocation; they do not yet establish a universal geographic map or rule out partial sharing of boundaries among species.

### Geographic distance remains the more robust spatial correlate

Continuous colour turnover had a consistently larger IBD-like association with geographic distance (partial rho approximately 0.022–0.028) than the BIO5-associated IBE-like component (approximately 0.008–0.010). The latter small association appeared in all three species-disjoint cohorts in raw photo-colour measurements. However, matched flower-minus-background validation and stringent same-observer sensitivities did not support a flower-specific or observer-independent BIO5 effect. Likewise, a directional claim that warmer places favour photographed white flowers failed cross-cohort transport. These results bound the role of climate: geographic separation is the more reproducible correlate, whereas temperature-mediated local adaptation remains a hypothesis rather than an established evolutionary explanation.

### Overall diversity and geographic organization capture different properties

The measurement design made these comparisons possible by describing species as sampled colour distributions rather than assigning each species one typical state. Observer-disjoint validation gave median Spearman rho = 0.789 for species-wide diversity D, while 136 species remeasured from new photographs showed rho = 0.968 and concordance correlation = 0.972 within the same source and measurement system. This provides meaningful reliability evidence, but not independent-source calibration or a global prevalence of truly polymorphic species.

Within the original disjoint species cohorts, greater sampled D was associated with stronger spatial colour organization after adjustment for sampled span and technical failures (partial rho = 0.127 in discovery, 0.099 in validation). The validation relationship persisted after a flower-minus-background contrast. Yet a *different* outcome, D versus 50-km local discordance depletion, did not reproduce in discovery (rho = 0.039, p = 0.135). The appropriate conclusion is that diversity and spatial organization are related but cannot be treated as interchangeable or governed by one scale-invariant relationship.

### Developmental accessibility and organ-specific costs are hypotheses to explain the axis

A repeated achromatic–chromatic axis might arise from repeatedly accessible biochemical or regulatory perturbations. In Silene littorea, Casimiro-Soriguer and colleagues found more than 42-fold lower F3h1 expression in white than pigmented buds and implicated Myb1a regulation, without finding a simple consistently segregating protein-coding variant across colour states. Independent work by Del Valle and colleagues distinguished petal anthocyanin loss (PAL) from whole-plant anthocyanin loss (WAL): the former occurred at 8–21% in two examined S. littorea populations, while the latter remained below 1% across surveyed populations. The existing FCP reanalysis of broader published PAL/WAL frequency tables is consistent with organ-limited white forms reaching higher observed frequencies, but it does not quantify the rate at which white variants originate, their mutation target size, their age or their fitness advantage.

These findings make pleiotropic costs a coherent candidate mechanism. Reducing pigmentation in petals while retaining protective pigmentation elsewhere could have different consequences from suppressing anthocyanins throughout the plant. However, visibly white flowers are not necessarily pigment-free. In Moricandia arvensis, seasonal plasticity generates lilac flowers in spring and visibly white flowers in the hot, dry summer, accompanied by changes in floral morphology, pollinator interactions and the profile of ultraviolet-absorbing compounds. These independent examples support the *biological plausibility* of pigment-network constraints, environmental responses and multifunctional flower traits; they do not identify a shared adaptive cause in the present species-wide photographic atlas.

This distinction also changes how a seemingly near-50:50 global photographic mixture should be interpreted. A similar aggregate fraction of photo-white and photo-coloured forms across broad latitude bands does not imply a stable 50:50 colour-morph equilibrium within individual populations. The original atlas mixes different photographed species, classified and unclassified images and spatially uneven observation opportunities. Estimating a benefit–cost equilibrium requires directly measuring organ-level pigments, independent genotype-linked colour states, pollination and herbivory outcomes and relative seed or pollen fitness.

### Generality with explicit boundaries

The strongest conclusion is therefore not that white flowers repeatedly evolve through one molecular loss pathway, nor that one temperature or rainfall coefficient selects the same colour in all flowering plants. Rather, species-wide photographed colour diversity repeatedly displays **geographic organization** and, separately, a **prospectively confirmed achromatic–chromatic direction of displacement**. These are nonredundant regularities: a common measured direction can coexist with heterogeneous spatial arrangements. Testing why will require designs that combine independently validated floral colour, within-population fitness and direct lineage histories across species that genuinely share comparable environments.

The present high-depth cohorts represent photo-sampling opportunities within one citizen-science measurement system, not a probability sample of all flowering plants. The lack of detectable broad tree-wide phylogenetic signal in D under tested placements does not establish the absence of finer lineage effects. Measurements of shared colour direction and local photo similarity also do not distinguish drift, dispersal, environmentally induced plasticity or selection. Those boundaries are not peripheral: they define why the present findings motivate mechanistic experiments rather than constitute an adaptive explanation.
