## Abstract

Large-scale associations between floral colour and climate may arise from differences in the species composing regional floras, local variation within species, shared evolutionary history, or the measurement and geography of biodiversity records. These explanations are not interchangeable. We assessed whether the *direction* of floral-colour–environment relationships generalizes across taxonomic weighting, spatial comparison and phenotype measurement in a globally distributed collection of community-science photographs. Our opportunity frame comprised 100,543 unique photographs from 42,111 named plant taxa; 45,953 photographs were assigned one of four visible colour categories. Among photographs with complete environmental covariates, we evaluated six interpretable climate axes, 36 measured abiotic variables jointly, and a separate source of text-derived botanical flower colours. In genus-conditioned comparisons among species, photographed white-colour probability increased with annual precipitation (+2.255 percentage points per global precipitation SD; within-scope, hue-by-climate simultaneous 95% interval [+0.319, +4.190]) and mean atmospheric vapour pressure (+3.952 [+1.199, +6.704]). These effects were not clearly supported in the strict-image-quality subset or when conditioning on genus within coarse geographic cells. Holding precisely the same 9,370 global species fixed, the difference between broad-geography and cell-conditioned precipitation slopes was +0.518 percentage points (24-comparison simultaneous interval [−1.253, +2.289]); the corresponding eastern-sector contrast also included zero after simultaneous adjustment. Independent botanical-description colour categories did not provide conclusive support for the previously selected eastern white–blue/purple contrast. Thus global photographed colour composition contains environmental information, but a universal directional response across floras or the causes of floral-colour variation are not established. Spatial pattern, environmental prediction and adaptive maintenance require distinct estimands and evidence.

## Introduction

Flowers provide visual signals in ecological contexts shaped by pollinators, herbivores, irradiance, temperature and resource availability. Studies of flower colour have therefore addressed both organismal and community functions, as well as abiotic gradients. An influential macroecological study of 339 species found associations of floral-colour variation with pollinator and plant communities, precipitation and solar radiation (Dalrymple et al., 2020). At the same time, flower colour can vary plastically with temperature and water availability, and genetic colour polymorphisms can be affected by selection, dispersal and demographic history (Narbona et al., 2018; Sapir et al., 2021; Lacey, 2026). These findings motivate large-scale environmental analyses but do not imply that a single direction of colour change is shared among lineages or across spatial scales.

A central inferential problem arises when colour is recorded from geographically structured biodiversity observations. A positive association between photographed white flowers and precipitation could mean that more species with white-looking flowers are encountered in rainy regions. Alternatively, some species may be more likely to express or retain white-flowered forms in wetter localities. Shared phylogenetic and regional history, the spatial distribution of sampling opportunities, phenology, differences in floral organs and camera exposure could also create or modify that association. A species-composition relationship is not a within-species reaction norm; neither is an observational environmental coefficient an estimate of natural selection. We refer to these different levels as **floristic composition**, **within-lineage geographic allocation**, **environmental predictive information**, and **environment-dependent relative fitness**.

Comparative scale can change the apparent strength of environmental relationships. Climate and biogeographic region covary, closely related species tend to occupy partially similar ranges, and environmental variables such as annual precipitation, atmospheric vapour pressure, solar radiation and soil water-holding properties are correlated. Restricting attention to congeneric species within the same coarse geographic region provides a different contrast from pooling all congeneric species across continents. However, such restrictions typically reduce the sample as well as changing the spatial adjustment. A more informative design must disentangle the loss of species coverage from the change in geographic conditioning on an identical set of observations.

Observation-derived colour presents an additional challenge. Community-science images can provide extensive geographic coverage, but illumination, highlighting, the selected flower organ, observer effort and classifiability vary across space (Laitly et al., 2021). A visual label obtained from an image is not necessarily a spectrophotometric hue, measured floral pigment concentration or a discrete heritable colour morph. Cross-checking with botanical descriptions offers a complementary phenotype measurement, but that comparison also changes which species and types of colour variation can be included. Failure to corroborate across measurement systems must therefore be reported without treating nonidentical denominators as a strict contradictory experiment.

Here we assemble the environmental component of the Flower Colour Polymorphism (FCP) project as a **single comparative macroecological analysis**, distinct from the programme's separate high-depth, within-species geographic-allocation manuscript. We ask: (1) Do jointly measured environments add predictive information about global photographed floral-colour composition beyond genus and coarse geographic covariates? (2) Which directions are observed for specific, ecologically interpretable climate axes and each of four photographed colour states? (3) Do those directions remain supported under strict image quality, constant species support and coarse within-region conditioning? (4) Does an independently extracted botanical-description phenotype corroborate the previously selected eastern white-versus-blue/purple precipitation direction? Our aim is to estimate the **reach and limitations of macroecological association**, not to establish the evolutionary origin or maintenance of flower-colour morphs.

## Materials and Methods

### Study design and evidence hierarchy

All environmental-direction analyses summarized here were devised **after** earlier FCP photographic analyses and are retrospective. Previously selected longitude sectors, floral-colour axes and related candidate environmental variables had already been examined. We therefore distinguish original-data numerical reproducibility, uncertainty conditional on specified comparisons, measurement-source corroboration and true external, prospective confirmation. None of the latter is implied by re-running code on the original source.

We organize the evidence into four layers. **Layer A** estimates how much joint climate/soil data improve the prediction of photographic four-colour compositions when a named genus and geographic features are already known. **Layer B** estimates signed climate-to-colour coefficients in the photographic source, with within-genus conditioning. **Layer C** fixes the species sample and compares broad geographic adjustment against genus-by-coarse-cell conditioning. **Layer D** repeats a related, but not numerically identical, colour–rainfall analysis using independently text-derived botanical flower-colour descriptions. The four layers answer different but linked questions; their estimates must not be pooled as independent replications or meta-analysed without reconciliation of their populations.

### Photographic and environmental source records

The union of original FCP geographical photo opportunities comprised **100,543 distinct photographs from 42,111 nominal taxa**. Of these, **45,953 (45.7%)** were classifiable into four previously fixed visible categories: **white, yellow/orange, red/pink and blue/purple**. The remaining **54,590** photos did not receive a four-state label and were **not** recoded as white or as a fifth biological state. The 42,111-taxon count is an opportunity frame, not an estimate of worldwide floral polymorphism prevalence, and the photograph records are not a globally representative probability sample.

Original photographs were joined by explicit photo, observation and taxon identifiers to environmental covariates evaluated at their recorded localities. The environmental inventory comprises 11 temperature variables, eight precipitation variables, two each for annual/seasonal solar radiation, wind and vapour pressure, one elevation variable and ten soil variables. **45,836 photographs / 25,028 taxa** have classified four-state colour and complete non-soil (26-variable) environmental information; **31,925 photographs / 20,786 taxa** also have the soil variables (36-variable environment). Different environmental completeness restrictions therefore change the taxonomic and geographical sample. We do not interpret between-sample differences in predictive scores as the causal effect of soil.

The two source archives were frozen under SHA-256 checksums `2d6097ba1b0434632e13bbe79dd78180608a287ff5dabebc770b5c0a2cfb52a0` (original photographic opportunities) and `6e287294b3a05eaa383be3de644551825915bfecca9c4bde6950b4890f5e16ca` (36-variable source environment). Source scripts and exact checksum validation are recorded in FCP Draft PRs #193 and #194; they do not alter the published/source images or the separate FCP New Phytologist manuscript.

### Multiclass colour responses and environmental predictors

For each nominal species in the eligible photographed population, we computed the proportions of its classifiable photographic records assigned to each of the four categories, so the vector of four sampled probabilities sums to one. Each named species provides a species-level response, while numbers of photographed records within a species determine measurement support rather than direct replication of evolution. We separately use **species-equal** weights and **genus-equal** weights, where the latter adjusts for the number of sampled species associated with a named genus.

The main interpretable axes were the warmest-month maximum temperature (WorldClim BIO5), annual precipitation (BIO12), driest-month precipitation (BIO14), precipitation seasonality (BIO15), mean annual solar radiation and mean annual atmospheric vapour pressure. In the full-36-data subset, an available-soil-water proxy was an additional candidate. These semantic axes were designated to avoid selecting only the largest observed coefficients, **but the designation occurred after earlier FCP outcomes had been viewed**. We cannot call the set preregistered or independently selected. Predictors were standardized with their overall source-population standard deviations within each environmental-completeness population; an individual regression coefficient is expressed as the **change in photographic colour probability, in percentage points, associated with one such global environmental SD**. Atmospheric vapour pressure is not relative humidity, vapour pressure deficit or directly measured plant drought stress; annual precipitation does not uniquely measure experienced water limitation.

### Joint environmental predictive information

A separate established FCP analysis compared models with genus-level photo-colour means and absolute latitude plus sine/cosine longitude covariates against models adding all 26 or 36 abiotic variables. Geographic fold assignment used species' source photographic locality centroids and 162 coarse original geographic cells. Training species within a genus supplied the genus colour baseline, and the environmental and geography-only models were evaluated on the same held-out source species. The target was the four-category Brier loss; an improvement is a decrease in **prediction error**, not a change in flower-colour frequency or explained adaptive fitness. Taxonomic weighting and genus-cluster bootstrap intervals were evaluated separately. The additional-prediction analysis is reported as an interpretive context for directional slopes because the same correlated variables can jointly carry predictive information without an identifiable, uniquely causal coefficient.

### Signed, genus-conditioned macroecological slopes

For each ecological axis, a separate genus-centred least-squares relationship estimated the direction for each of the four photographic colour fractions while retaining absolute latitude, sine longitude and cosine longitude. Thus a coefficient describes the **within-genus, between-named-species** gradient, conditional on those *coarse* coordinates, not an independent effect after controlling for every other abiotic axis. We also computed exploratory simultaneous 26/36-predictor ridge coefficients but did not use signs of highly collinear partial estimates as evidence of a specific selection mechanism.

Photographic observations were evaluated within five geographical scopes: the world and four fixed 90° longitude sectors (`[−180°,−90°)`, `[−90°,0°)`, `[0°,90°)` and `[90°,180°]`). Full-classifiable and technical strict-quality subsets were separated. The existing strict-quality rule required at least **23,826 effective flower pixels** and an extraneous/nuisance fraction **no greater than 0.1375717742**. The 26- and 36-variable source cohorts, the two photographic-quality cohorts and the five geographical scopes produced **20 source cohort conditions**. With both species/genus weightings and an additional fixed spatial-support sensitivity, the archived analysis records **1,280 directional estimates**. Sample restrictions, missing classes and unequal genus coverage are explicitly reported rather than filled by favourable substitution.

For every eligible cohort/weight, 999 matched-genus bootstrap resamples were applied jointly to all its candidate axes and hues. The 95% critical value of the maximum standardized centred bootstrap deviation generated a **within-cohort** approximate simultaneous confidence interval over the six (or seven) axes × four hues. It does not correct selection across all prior exploratory hypotheses, the five geographical scopes, technical filters, analysis families or attempts. Confidence intervals should therefore be read as conditional sampling sensitivities, not discovery-proof familywise tests across the entire historical programme.

### Matched-sample comparison of spatial conditioning

We identified nominal species belonging to genus-by-original-coarse-geographic-cell combinations with at least three species. For the global and previously emphasized eastern sector (90–180° E), we fitted three explicitly distinct models: **A**, all eligible source species belonging to qualifying genera, adjusted for broad geographic coordinates; **B**, the qualifying *species subset* adjusted for the same broad coordinates; and **C**, *exactly the same species, original colour proportions and environmental averages as B*, but with within-genus-by-coarse-cell centring replacing the broad coordinate adjustment. A–B measures support/species-composition changes within selected genera; **B–C compares spatial-conditioning specifications on an identical species set**. B–C does **not** estimate the causal effect of space because the models condition on different sets of geographic information and may differ in environmental variance remaining.

The same 999 genus-resampling draws were used to estimate the B–C paired difference. We report nominal and within-comparison-family (six axes × four hues) approximate maxT 95% intervals. The global fixed B/C subset contained **9,370 species, 845 genera and 14,268 photographed records**; the eastern fixed subset contained **2,886 species, 280 genera and 4,149 photographed records**. The local comparison used coarse equal-area geographic cells, not exact 50-km matching, a geodesic Gaussian process or an explicit dated phylogeny.

### Independent botanical-description measurement diagnostic

To avoid using the same photographic classifier for all evidence, we used the Natural History Museum's independently derived flower-colour descriptions compiled from botanical literature (Araujo et al., 2026). The source contained **59,369 named botanical records**. We retained strict binomial matches for which available flower-, petal- and corolla-colour fields consistently denoted a single unambiguous **white** or **blue/purple/violet** class. Mixed hues, conflicting organ descriptions and unclassifiable names were excluded rather than reinterpreted from FCP photographic results.

This strictly binary, **species-level descriptive** response cannot reproduce the denominator of the four-hue photo response or represent local within-species morph-frequency change. Moreover, *environmental sampling locations still came from the original FCP photograph opportunity data*, including records whose photographic colour was unclassified. Consequently, this is independence of **phenotype measurement**, not independent species-occurrence or microclimatic sampling. The exact source matching yielded **2,476 described white/blue species from 1,144 genera and 8,120 FCP photographic site records**. The east-versus-rest matched analysis retained **58 genera, 670 named species and 2,355 photographic site records**, with fixed eligibility of at least three colour-labelled species per geographical side in each genus. Genus bootstrap intervals, two species/genus weightings, and a four-sector comparison family were reported. The eastern white–blue contrast had been selected in earlier photographic work and thus remains explicitly post hoc.

### Analytical scope and causal non-identification

None of the four evidence layers measures individual plants' heritable colour genotypes, pigment synthesis, pollinator or herbivore fitness effects, survival, seed set or long-term morph-frequency dynamics. Genus centring is not a phylogenetic comparative model with branch-length covariance. Photographs do not provide a standardised count of independent genets or natural populations, and locality distributions may reflect observer/collector effort. We therefore do **not** identify the selective mechanism that generated regional colour compositions, much less the maintenance of within-population genetic polymorphisms.

## Results

### Joint abiotic conditions contain small additional predictive information

In the global 26-variable source cohort, adding the joint environment to a genus/geography-only photographic-colour predictor reduced the genus-weighted four-colour Brier loss by **0.002943** (cluster 95% interval **[0.001924, 0.004109]**) among **17,183 evaluated species**. In the 36-variable soil-complete cohort, the corresponding gain was **0.001432** **[0.000169, 0.002786]** among **13,961 evaluated species**. The full-36 gain represented approximately **0.3% of the genus-weighted geographical baseline loss**. The two completeness cohorts are not the same species set. The joint environmental model therefore contains modest conditional predictive information, but these Brier differences neither allocate that information uniquely among covarying drivers nor identify a universal colour–climate slope.

In the stronger extrapolation exercise, excluding both test genera and the test longitude sector plus 500 km reduced the full-36-variable environment's genus-weighted improvement to **0.000174** (95% interval **[−0.002629, 0.002846]**), compared with **0.004312 [0.002174, 0.006357]** under genus-only exclusion. Holding out distant floras changes training membership and environmental support as well as geography, preventing identification of a region-specific selective mechanism from that reduction alone. These results place a limit on the transportability of global environment-based prediction.

### At broad macroecological scales, wetter photographed floras show a positive white-colour association

The genus-equal global 26-variable source scope included **20,694 photograph-classified species from 1,934 qualifying genera** for the direction analysis. Under broad geographic adjustment, the annual precipitation (BIO12) coefficient for the photographed **white** category was **+2.255 percentage points per source-global BIO12 SD**, with a within-scope hue-by-anchor approximate maxT 95% interval of **[+0.319, +4.190]**. Mean atmospheric vapour pressure had a white association of **+3.952 [+1.199, +6.704]** points per respective environmental SD. The positive white probability is balanced by the other three category coefficients (for BIO12: yellow/orange −1.221, red/pink −0.446, blue/purple −0.588 points), but none of those three BIO12 hue coefficients independently excluded zero under the same within-scope maxT family. These are associations with photographed visible labels among congeneric **species**; they do not imply that a given species turns white in wetter environments.

Other tested directional hypotheses were not similarly supported under the same whole-world genus-equal family. Warmest-month BIO5 had a white coefficient **+1.257 [−0.648, +3.161]**, and annual solar radiation **−0.533 [−2.944, +1.878]**. In the 36-variable soil-complete source, a precipitation-to-white association **+2.257 [+0.138, +4.376]** and a vapour-pressure-to-white association **+3.987 [+1.038, +6.936]** were also observed, with a soil available-water proxy association **+1.698 [+0.003, +3.393]**. The soil-based association lies on a different complete-case sample and is not evidence that soil independently causes pigmentation loss.

The previously explored eastern longitude sector showed a stronger broad-scope precipitation–white coefficient (**+4.716 [+1.565, +7.866]**), but the eastern sector was emphasized after earlier source observations and cannot be treated as a geographically preregistered discovery. These statements refer to **the specified hue-by-environment family within each scope** and are not corrected for historical screening of regions and endpoints.

### The signed association is not robust to image quality or coarse within-region conditioning

When global photographic eligibility was restricted to the strict technical-quality subset, the precipitation–white estimate was **+1.229 [−2.046, +4.504]** points per global source SD (5,848 eligible species, 727 genera). For a within-genus **and within-original-coarse-cell** analysis, the corresponding estimate was **+0.808 [−3.556, +5.172]** points (9,370 species, 845 genera). Neither interval excluded zero under its defined within-scope hue-by-anchor simultaneous adjustment; among the six climate axes × four colours, no genus-equal within-cell interval excluded zero in the global subset. The eastern sector displayed a similar loss of clear directional support: +4.716 points in broad comparison versus **−2.076 [−8.794, +4.643]** points in genus-by-cell conditioning, with notably different eligible species unless they are explicitly matched.

### Fixed-species comparisons separate changing coverage from changing geographic conditioning

We then held identical species, photographs and four-colour outcomes constant across two alternative spatial specifications. For the global BIO12–white coefficient under genus-equal weighting, the broad-geography estimate was **+1.326** points on the fixed B species set, compared with **+0.808** points after cell conditioning (C). The paired B–C difference was **+0.518** points, with a within-scope 24-contrast maxT 95% interval **[−1.253, +2.289]**. In the east (2,886 fixed species), B yielded **+1.102** points and C **−2.076** points: B–C = **+3.178**, nominal genus-bootstrap 95% interval **[+1.077, +5.351]**, but simultaneous interval **[−0.258, +6.613]**.

The samples alone therefore cannot account for every change in the point estimate when conditioning differs. However, **neither genus-equal B–C difference excluded zero under the specified 24-comparison simultaneous intervals**; neither is a causal estimate of removing a confounder. The full three-stage comparison is provided in Table 2, rather than selected as a newly positive climatic driver.

### Independent botanical descriptions do not establish the eastern direction

The original source-text botanical survey included 59,369 named records. Under strict single-colour coding and exact binomial matching, the FCP site opportunity sample yielded 2,476 independently botanically labelled white or blue/purple species. The prespecified within-analysis east-versus-rest matched subset had 58 genera and 670 species. The eastern precipitation white-minus-blue/purple slope difference was **+2.07** percentage points for species-equal weighting (genus-bootstrap 95% interval **[−13.87, +19.07]**) and **+3.56** for genus-equal weighting **[−15.34, +22.60]**. All eastern four-sector simultaneous intervals included zero, as did the primary nominal intervals.

The independent phenotype measurement therefore **did not corroborate the eastern photo-colour signal at the fixed support criterion**. This is neither a statistical demonstration of the opposite direction nor a fully independent direct replication. The more restricted binary botanical species phenotype and continued reuse of FCP observation-site climates constrain what can be learned. A global climatic-selection rule is not supported by this evidence.

## Discussion

### Broad floral-colour–moisture correspondence is detectable, but its ecological level matters

The most concrete positive result of this analysis is a directional **between-species** macroecological association: within nominal genera and after coarse geographic adjustment, regions with higher annual precipitation or atmospheric vapour pressure tend to contribute more photographs categorised as white. Adding a suite of correlated abiotic variables also improves prediction of the observed four-colour composition beyond simple genus/geography baselines, although by a small absolute amount. These results identify patterns in sampled floral-colour composition that merit ecological investigation. They do not establish a single environmental pressure, a common plastic response, or a selective advantage of white flowers.

Our findings complement, rather than supersede, Dalrymple et al. (2020), who showed that macroecological flower-colour traits covary with both biotic and abiotic conditions. Their study used a different quantitative colour metric, geographical sample and set of taxa; its climatic associations cannot be treated as a direct replication of our photographic white-category coefficient. The new emphasis here is the **transfer of inferential level**: an association evident among photographed congeneric species need not be recovered with a more local geographic contrast, a stricter source-image subset or a distinct measurement of flower colour.

### Broad environments, species composition and spatial support are entangled

Spatial extent determines which taxa and environments are compared. At continental scale, a model can combine differences among congeneric species occupying dissimilar climate regimes and longitudes. Conditioning on the same genus within a coarse geographic cell removes much of that between-region contrast while retaining only cells with sufficient congeneric sampling. Our three-stage analysis improves on an unpaired comparison by matching the species exactly for B and C. It shows that changing the spatial specification can shift the fitted precipitation–white direction without changing the biological/photo observations included. Nonetheless, the paired difference does not pass simultaneous inference for the primary genus-equal comparisons. This result should therefore be described as **sensitivity of the fitted environmental association to spatial conditioning**, not proof that geography is the underlying evolutionary cause or that all apparent moisture effects are spatial artefacts.

The species-level geographic data are also unbalanced: some regions include many more sampled species and images than others, the 90° longitude regions are arbitrary rather than biological provinces, and a coarse-cell analysis is not a fine-scale local adaptation design. A more local conditioning set reduces remaining climatic variation and may lower power. Failure to reject a null slope within restricted cells is not evidence that a physiological reaction norm is exactly zero. Conversely, a positive large-scale photographic slope does not demonstrate independent within-population selection. Both statistical statements can be true under geographically structured trait composition.

### Measurement dependence limits cross-source ecological claims

Photo-colour categorisation is useful at large geographic scale but subject to colour rendering, exposure, organ selection and selection into the classifiable category. More than half of original photo opportunities were not classifiable into the four-state scheme; such missingness need not be random with respect to species, geography or climate. Previous FCP image-validity tests found substantial coupling between digital near-clipping and the coarse white classification in a separate high-depth cohort; we do not assume that specific numerical effect transfers to all global low-depth photos, but it reinforces the importance of measurement sensitivity. The failure to recover a clear gradient in the strict-quality subset leaves both reduced precision and technical/biological selection as possibilities.

The Natural History Museum trait descriptions add a genuinely different colour-measurement process but not an identical outcome. A description such as 'white' or 'violet' often represents the species rather than the geographic distribution of its local morphs. Excluding mixed-colour species further restricts the biological system most relevant to within-species polymorphism. The inconclusive external-source interval demonstrates the current **lack of convergent support**, not a definitive falsification of every possible colour–climate association. For independent confirmation, both botanically verified petal/corolla colour and sampling environments would need independent, defined population/specimen sites; the latter was not achieved by the description-only comparison here.

### Prediction and selection are different inferential objectives

A predictive reduction in four-colour Brier loss answers whether environmental information helps discriminate colour compositions under a particular geographical data and cross-validation design. A signed coefficient answers how photo-colour probability varies along a measured environmental gradient within the sampled comparison population. An evolutionary selection coefficient would instead require colour-specific **relative survival or reproduction**, suitably associated with climate and controlled for population history, nonrandom genotype placement, phenotypic plasticity and correlated reproductive traits. Neither a positive Brier gain nor a signed macroecological coefficient provides this causal quantity. Similarly, genus-level matching does not replace a dated phylogenetic covariance model, and a climate raster value at a photo site does not represent experimental plant-level heat or water exposure.

The independent main FCP manuscript on range-wide **within-species** colour allocation reports repeated local colour homogeneity relative to species-specific composition-preserving nulls across species-disjoint high-depth photo cohorts. Those results address *where sampled intraspecific variation is arranged*, not whether a general macroclimate direction determines its evolution. This manuscript addresses a different, comparatively low-depth, broad-taxon environmental estimand. Because both arise from the FCP/iNaturalist measurement programme, overlapping photographs, algorithms and opportunities must be disclosed before either manuscript is submitted. The high-depth results are not presented here as an independently replicated macroclimate effect or reproduced as a second paper's principal data analysis.

### Implications for world maps and maintenance of colour polymorphism

A world map depicting classified photo colours might be a valuable **descriptive, observation-process-conditioned atlas**, especially if coverage, missingness and species-equal weighting are made visible. It is not automatically a map of natural colour-morph frequencies, nor evidence that species share the same geographic colour-transition boundaries. Repeated species-conditioned re-mapping, uncertainty-aware hotspot probabilities and comparison with spatially structured nulls would be required before claiming a shared global flower-colour geography. Our environment-direction analysis does not establish such an atlas or universal boundary.

Explaining the adaptive origin or genetic maintenance of colour polymorphism requires a different design. Distinguishing locally balancing selection, spatially varying selection, genetic drift, historical isolation, phenotypic plasticity, frequency dependence and gene flow would require measured local morph and genotype frequencies, replicated temporal population sampling, and potentially reciprocal transplants or manipulations measuring phenotype-specific fitness under contrasting environments (Delph & Kelly, 2014; Sapir et al., 2021; Schemske & Bierzychudek, 2007). These quantities are missing from the global photographs and text-derived species descriptions. A climatic selective mechanism should therefore remain a hypothesis even when the same broad-scale environment–colour association is seen in multiple regions.

### Strengths, limitations and research priorities

The strengths of the study are a common worldwide four-category photographic measurement framework, a genus- and geography-conditioned comparison, explicitly retained failures and unclassifiable records, numerical control of multiple colour–environment contrasts within defined families, a species-fixed spatial decomposition and independent botanical-colour measurement. Its limitations are substantial: nonrandom photographic selection, an imperfect visible-colour proxy, high classifiability missingness, incomplete direct phylogenetic control, differing source cohorts under soil and image-quality restrictions, arbitrary longitude sectors and a retrospective sequence of hypothesis refinements. Bootstrap intervals condition on the specified source, fitted comparison and selected families, not on the full selection history. Our cross-source diagnostic is also not an independent occurrence-based replication.

The next decisive analysis is not another search for favourable combinations of the 36 abiotic variables. It is a **source-independent, geographically explicit test with colour verified before outcomes are evaluated**, sufficient congeneric/phylogenetic replication, documented environmental analogue support, robust handling of mixed-colour species and explicitly fixed comparison regions. The separate proposed preserved-specimen study presently has only a frozen botanical-species sampling manifest; no GBIF/WorldClim-colour ecological result is claimed for that design. If those data do not provide sufficient common environmental support, the correct output is an identifiability limitation rather than a revised climatic headline.

## Conclusions

Among photographed species distributed around the world, precipitation and atmospheric water vapour covary positively with the sampled probability of being classified as white after coarse genus and geographic adjustment. A full multivariate environmental predictor carries additional, albeit small, information. The direction is not consistently established under stricter photographic-quality restrictions, geographically local congeners or a separate botanical-description phenotype. These contrasts show why large-scale trait maps, ecological predictions, environmental selection and the genetic maintenance of morphs must be investigated as separate quantities. **Global macroecological association is demonstrable in the present photographic sample; a universal climatic colour response and its evolutionary mechanism are not.**
