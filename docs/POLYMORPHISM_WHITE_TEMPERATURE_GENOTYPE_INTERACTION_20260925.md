# Cross-system genotype/morph × temperature evidence — 2026-09-25

## Conclusion

The cross-system evidence rejects a simple universal rule:

`
higher temperature -> white flowers
`

The stronger general hypothesis is:

`
pigment-network genotype / regulatory state × temperature
    -> pigment output and/or fitness
    -> context-dependent colour-state sorting
`

Temperature repeatedly interacts with the same biochemical system, but the phenotypic or fitness direction differs among species.

## Systems

### Moricandia arvensis — heat changes colour state within genotype

Public source-data reanalysis shows a strong temperature dose response under the same stated summer photoperiod.

Mild summer -> hotter summer:
- 15 individuals;
- cyanidin mean 1.893 -> 0.531;
- ~71.9% reduction;
- 13/15 decrease;
- paired p=0.000854.

Returning plants toward spring conditions restores cyanidin.

The summer-white transcriptome also downregulates FCP-overlap nodes MYB90, CHS, U75C1/U78D2 and TTG1.

Role: **environmental perturbation can move the pigment network toward an achromatic state.**

### Ipomoea purpurea — a natural pigment lesion changes heat fitness

The natural white `a` allele is a CHS-D null caused by a transposon insertion.

A full-factorial phytotron experiment used wild-derived, background-randomized AA pigmented and aa white genotypes under low/high temperature and low/high light.

At high temperature:
- white aa pollen recipients had ~26% lower fertilization success than AA;
- aa pollen had ~24% lower fertilization success than AA in high-temperature recipient environments.

At low temperature the corresponding fertilization genotype disadvantage was absent.

Light did not produce the same fertilization interaction.

Role: **the same pathway lesion that makes flowers white also changes the biological cost of heat.**

This is a direct genotype × temperature example, but the outcome is reproductive fitness rather than further colour change.

### Parrya nudicaulis — natural climate × pathway bridge

Across 14 Alaskan populations, white-flower frequency increases with growing-season temperature.

White petals show a strong petal-specific reduction in CHS expression near the entry to the anthocyanin biosynthetic pathway.

Role: **natural temperature sorting covaries with a known pigment-network mechanism in the same species.**

Because the climate contrast is observational, it does not prove temperature is the causal selective agent.

### Ophiorrhiza japonica — morph × temperature with the opposite ecological direction

Natural pink and white morphs differ across seasonal and experimental thermal conditions.

The published interpretation is that pink morphs perform better in warmer conditions, whereas white morphs are favored toward colder/winter conditions; pollinator visitation does not explain the dimorphism.

Role: **temperature interacts with colour morph, but not with a universal hotter->white sign.**

## Synthesis

These systems separate three processes that are often conflated:

1. **phenotype production** — heat can directly suppress anthocyanin and produce a white phenotype (*Moricandia*);
2. **pleiotropic fitness cost** — loss of a pigment-pathway enzyme can alter heat tolerance (*Ipomoea*);
3. **spatial/temporal sorting** — colour morph frequencies can track thermal environment (*Parrya*, *Ophiorrhiza*).

The cross-system commonality is the **temperature sensitivity of pigment-network states**, not one universal direction of colour change.

## FCP implication

This provides a mechanistic explanation for why:
- the white/nonwhite phenotype-space axis can recur across species;
- natural white systems repeatedly involve pigment-network perturbations;
- a prospective BIO5 association can appear in one cohort but fail to replicate in others.

If genotype distributions and thermal response functions differ among species, a universal temperature main effect is not expected even when temperature is mechanistically important.

## Next decisive comparative prediction

For species with natural white/pigmented polymorphism and a known pigment-pathway lesion/regulatory difference:

`
colour genotype/morph × experimental temperature
`

should explain pigment concentration and/or fitness better than temperature alone.

The sign of the temperature main effect is allowed to vary by species.

A cross-species hierarchical experiment should therefore estimate:
- species-specific temperature slope;
- genotype/morph-specific temperature slope;
- variance of the genotype × temperature interaction;
- association of interaction magnitude with the affected pigment-network node.

## Claim ceiling

Supported as a working synthesis:
- pigment-network states repeatedly interact with temperature across independent systems.

Not supported:
- universal hotter -> white direction;
- universal selection for white under heat;
- universal anthocyanin mechanism across all angiosperm pigment systems;
- global BIO5 as a replicated FCP driver.
